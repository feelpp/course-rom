[[inner-products]]
== Inner products and induced norms

A real vector space is closed under sums and scalar multiplication. An *inner product* stem:[(w,v)_V] is bilinear, symmetric, and positive definite: stem:[(v,v)_V>0] for nonzero stem:[v]. It defines

[stem]
++++
\|v\|_V=\sqrt{(v,v)_V},\qquad
|(w,v)_V|\leq\|w\|_V\|v\|_V.
++++

The second formula is Cauchy–Schwarz. A *Hilbert space* is an inner-product space complete in this norm: limits of Cauchy sequences remain in the space. Every finite-dimensional inner-product space is complete.

If stem:[(w,v)_V=0], the vectors are orthogonal. Expanding the square gives Pythagoras:

[stem]
++++
\|w+v\|_V^2=\|w\|_V^2+\|v\|_V^2.
++++

This identity explains why a POD reconstruction separates captured energy from discarded energy. Here energy means the chosen *squared norm*, not necessarily physical heat.

In coordinates, a symmetric positive-definite Gram matrix stem:[G] gives stem:[(w,v)_V=\mathbf w^TG\mathbf v]. The Euclidean choice stem:[G=I], a mass matrix and a reference PDE matrix define different norms. A basis is stem:[G]-orthonormal when stem:[Z^TGZ=I_r].

[[function-spaces]]
== Functions, boundary conditions and coordinates

For a spatial domain stem:[\Omega], stem:[L^2(\Omega)] contains square-integrable functions, and stem:[H^1(\Omega)] additionally requires square-integrable weak first derivatives. These spaces let us express diffusion problems without requiring pointwise second derivatives.
The trial space imposes the essential boundary condition. In our bar example,

[stem]
++++
V=\{v\in H^1(0,1):v(0)=0\}.
++++

A finite-element subspace stem:[V_h\subset V] has basis functions stem:[\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_n]. A discrete *function* and its coordinate vector are related by

[stem]
++++
u_h(x;\mu)=\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbf u_i(\mu)\phi_i(x),
\qquad \mathbf u(\mu)\in\mathbb R^n.
++++

The vectors in the columns of stem:[Z\in\mathbb R^{n\times r}] likewise represent reduced basis functions stem:[\zeta_j=\sum_i Z_{ij}\phi_i]. Thus stem:[u_r=\sum_j\mathbf a_j\zeta_j] has full-order coordinates stem:[\mathbf u_r=Z\mathbf a]. The reduced coefficients stem:[\mathbf a] are not a spatial field.

[[weak-problem]]
== Linear functionals, bilinear forms and the weak PDE

A *linear functional* stem:[f:V\to\mathbb R] returns one scalar from a function, for example an integral or a sensor reading. It is bounded when stem:[|f(v)|\leq C\|v\|_V]. The dual space stem:[V'] contains bounded linear functionals.
A *bilinear form* stem:[a_\mu(w,v)] is linear separately in its two function arguments. Its parameter dependence need not be linear.

For the stationary thermal practical, integration by parts gives

[stem]
++++
a_{\mu,g}(w,v)=a_\mu(w,v)
=\mu\int_0^1w'v'+\int_0^1wv,
\qquad f_g(v)=\int_0^1v+g\,v(1),
++++

and the weak equation is stem:[a_\mu(u_h,v)=f_g(v)] for every stem:[v\in V_h]. The positive endpoint term corresponds to the prescribed inward flux stem:[\mu u'(1)=g]. The flux changes the load, not the operator. Point evaluation is bounded in this one-dimensional stem:[H^1] setting; that property should not be assumed for arbitrary function spaces.

In matrix form, stem:[A(\mu)\mathbf u=b+g e_n] with stem:[A(\mu)=\mu K+M]. The practical uses a lumped mass quadrature for stem:[M] and the integral load; its matrix identities and certificates refer to that declared discrete model.

An output stem:[s_h=\ell(u_h)] is *compliant* when stem:[\ell=f_g]. A mean temperature stem:[\int_0^1u_h] is a useful output, but is generally not compliant when the endpoint load term is present. Compliance is a relation between the output and the complete load, not a synonym for linearity.

[[energy-and-stability]]
== Symmetry, coercivity and the two energy norms

Symmetry means stem:[a_\mu(w,v)=a_\mu(v,w)]. Coercivity and continuity in a declared *fixed* norm mean

[stem]
++++
a_\mu(v,v)\geq\alpha(\mu)\|v\|_V^2,\qquad
|a_\mu(w,v)|\leq\gamma(\mu)\|w\|_V\|v\|_V,
\qquad \alpha(\mu)>0.
++++

For a symmetric coercive form, the current-parameter energy norm is

[stem]
++++
\lvert\!\lvert\!\lvert v\rvert\!\rvert\!\rvert_\mu
=\|v\|_{a_\mu}=\sqrt{a_\mu(v,v)}.
++++

Fixing stem:[\bar\mu] defines a reference norm stem:[\|v\|_V=\|v\|_{a_{\bar\mu}}]. For the thermal practical, stem:[\bar\mu=1] and stem:[G=K+M]. The inequalities above imply

[stem]
++++
\sqrt{\alpha(\mu)}\|v\|_V
\leq\|v\|_{a_\mu}\leq\sqrt{\gamma(\mu)}\|v\|_V.
++++

These norm comparisons are the bridge used in xref:certification/residual-bound.adoc[the error-bound proofs]. They do not identify the two norms away from the reference parameter. For a nonsymmetric form, stem:[a_\mu] is not an inner product; the symmetric-case proofs require adaptation.

[[residual-and-projection]]
== Orthogonality, residual and error

Galerkin tests the weak PDE only against stem:[V_r]. Subtracting the full and reduced equations gives

[stem]
++++
a_\mu(u_h-u_r,v_r)=0\quad(v_r\in V_r).
++++

For a symmetric form, this is orthogonality of the error to the reduced space in the *current-parameter energy inner product*. It differs from the fixed-metric projection used to reconstruct training snapshots in POD.

The residual functional stem:[R_\mu(v)=f_\mu(v)-a_\mu(u_r,v)] vanishes on stem:[V_r], but generally does not vanish on stem:[V_h]. It satisfies stem:[R_\mu(v)=a_\mu(e,v)] for stem:[e=u_h-u_r]. Its fixed dual norm is

[stem]
++++
\|R_\mu\|_{V_h'}=\sup_{v\in V_h\setminus\{0\}}
\frac{|R_\mu(v)|}{\|v\|_V}.
++++

[[riesz-supremum]]
=== Why does a Riesz solve compute the supremum?

The xref:foundations/riesz.adoc#riesz-theorem[Riesz representation theorem] associates one vector stem:[z_\mu] with the functional:

[stem]
++++
(z_\mu,v)_V=R_\mu(v)\quad\text{for every }v\in V_h.
++++

The important point is not just that this vector bounds the supremum: *it gives a direction that attains it*. For a nonzero functional, the unit direction stem:[v_\star=z_\mu/\|z_\mu\|_V] is a maximizer. Solving for stem:[z_\mu] therefore replaces searching over all possible directions.

[.prop,caption=""]
.Proposition — The Riesz representative identifies the maximizing direction
====
If stem:[R_\mu\ne0], then

[stem]
++++
\|v_\star\|_V=1,\qquad
|R_\mu(v_\star)|=R_\mu(v_\star)=\|z_\mu\|_V
=\sup_{v\ne0}\frac{|R_\mu(v)|}{\|v\|_V}.
++++

If stem:[R_\mu=0], its representative and its dual norm are both zero.
====

[%collapsible.proof,caption=""]
.Proof — First bound every direction, then attain the bound
====
*Upper bound.* Cauchy–Schwarz gives, for every nonzero stem:[v],

[stem]
++++
\frac{|R_\mu(v)|}{\|v\|_V}
=\frac{|(z_\mu,v)_V|}{\|v\|_V}
\leq\frac{\|z_\mu\|_V\|v\|_V}{\|v\|_V}
=\|z_\mu\|_V.
++++

Thus the supremum cannot exceed stem:[\|z_\mu\|_V]. An upper bound alone would not establish equality.

*Attainment.* If stem:[z_\mu\ne0], choose stem:[v=z_\mu]. The quotient becomes

[stem]
++++
\frac{|R_\mu(z_\mu)|}{\|z_\mu\|_V}
=\frac{(z_\mu,z_\mu)_V}{\|z_\mu\|_V}
=\frac{\|z_\mu\|_V^2}{\|z_\mu\|_V}
=\|z_\mu\|_V.
++++

Normalizing this vector gives stem:[v_\star] with unit norm and the same quotient. We have both an upper bound for every direction and a direction achieving it, so the supremum equals that bound. If stem:[z_\mu=0], its inner product with every vector is zero, hence the functional and the supremum vanish. No normalized maximizing vector is needed in that case.
====

In coordinates, stem:[R_\mu(v)=\boldsymbol\rho^T\mathbf v] and stem:[(z_\mu,v)_V=\mathbf z_\mu^TG\mathbf v]. Equality for every stem:[\mathbf v] gives the xref:foundations/riesz.adoc#matrix-riesz[Riesz linear system]

[stem]
++++
G\mathbf z_\mu=\boldsymbol\rho,\qquad
\|R_\mu\|_{V_h'}=\sqrt{\mathbf z_\mu^TG\mathbf z_\mu}
=\sqrt{\boldsymbol\rho^T\mathbf z_\mu}.
++++

For example, stem:[G=\operatorname{diag}(4,1)] and stem:[\boldsymbol\rho=(2,1)^T] give stem:[\mathbf z_\mu=(1/2,1)^T]. The dual norm is stem:[\sqrt2], attained at stem:[\mathbf v_\star=(1/2,1)^T/\sqrt2]. The Euclidean norm of the residual coefficient vector would be stem:[\sqrt5], which is a different quantity. Solve the linear system; do not form an explicit inverse.

[[affine-structure]]
== Affine structure and superposition

*Purpose: reuse parameter-independent work when parameters change.* For the *full finite-element model*, assemble fixed components once, then combine them for each parameter. This principle does not require model reduction.

An affine decomposition separates scalar parameter functions from fixed forms. Include the operator, the load *and the linear output*:

[stem]
++++
a_\mu(w,v)=\sum_{q=1}^{Q_a}\Theta_q^a(\mu)a_q(w,v),
\qquad f_\mu(v)=\sum_{q=1}^{Q_f}\Theta_q^f(\mu)f_q(v),
++++

[stem]
++++
\ell_\mu(v)=\sum_{q=1}^{Q_\ell}\Theta_q^\ell(\mu)\ell_q(v),
\qquad s_h(\mu)=\ell_\mu(u_h(\mu)).
++++

The forms stem:[a_q,f_q,\ell_q] do not depend on the query parameter. On a fixed finite-element space their matrices and vectors are

[stem]
++++
A_q\in\mathbb R^{n\times n},\qquad f_q,\boldsymbol\ell_q\in\mathbb R^n.
++++

The vector stem:[\boldsymbol\ell_q] represents the functional through stem:[\ell_q(v)=\boldsymbol\ell_q^T\mathbf v]. An output is not generally the load: its components and coefficient functions can be different.

*Parameter-independent does not mean small.* These arrays still depend on the finite-element space and its dimension stem:[n]; assembling and manipulating them can be expensive. Independence from stem:[\mu] makes them reusable, not reduced. The functions stem:[\Theta_q(\mu)] are scalars and may depend nonlinearly on the parameter.

=== Faster assembly for the full finite-element model

Perform the element integration and assembly for each fixed component once. At a new parameter, form

[stem]
++++
A(\mu)=\sum_q\Theta_q^a(\mu)A_q,\qquad
\mathbf f(\mu)=\sum_q\Theta_q^f(\mu)f_q,\qquad
\boldsymbol\ell(\mu)=\sum_q\Theta_q^\ell(\mu)\boldsymbol\ell_q.
++++

This replaces repeated element integration by linear combinations of stored matrices and vectors. *No reduced basis is needed for this benefit.* The assembled arrays still have full dimension: sparse matrix combinations and the solve stem:[A(\mu)\mathbf u=\mathbf f(\mu)] still involve stem:[n], and evaluating stem:[s_h=\boldsymbol\ell(\mu)^T\mathbf u] is still a full-order operation. Affine separation speeds up assembly; it does not by itself make the finite-element solve reduced-dimensional.

For sparse components, combination work scales with their stored entries; for dense components it is of order stem:[Q_a n^2]. The gain depends on the number of affine terms and the cost of element integration versus these combinations.

[cols="1,3,2",options="header"]
|===
|Stage |What is computed? |Size and reuse
|Precompute finite-element components
|Assemble fixed stem:[A_q], stem:[f_q] and stem:[\boldsymbol\ell_q] once.
|stem:[n\times n] matrices and length-stem:[n] vectors; reusable across parameter queries.
|At a new parameter
|Combine the fixed components, solve the full system and evaluate stem:[s_h=\boldsymbol\ell(\mu)^T\mathbf u].
|Faster assembly, but the matrix, solve and output still involve stem:[n].
|===

=== Thermal example — operator, load and output

Here stem:[A(\mu)=\mu K+M] and stem:[\mathbf f(g)=b+g e_n]. The matrices stem:[K,M] and vectors stem:[b,e_n] depend on the finite-element mesh, but not on stem:[\mu] or stem:[g]. A full-order solver can assemble them once, then form stem:[\mu K+M] and stem:[b+g e_n] for each parameter pair. If only stem:[g] changes, the operator is unchanged and its factorization can also be reused.

The two full-order example outputs are:

* *Mean temperature:* stem:[\ell(v)=b^T\mathbf v] is parameter independent, so its assembled output vector stem:[b] can be stored once. Evaluate stem:[s_h=b^T\mathbf u]. This is not the full load functional when stem:[g\ne0].
* *Compliant output:* stem:[\ell_g(v)=f_g(v)=(b+g e_n)^T\mathbf v] has two fixed affine components with weights stem:[(1,g)]. Evaluate stem:[s_h^{\mathrm{comp}}=(b+g e_n)^T\mathbf u].

Changing the mesh requires updating these finite-element components.

*Later in the course:* the xref:reduction/galerkin.adoc#affine-offline-online[offline/online decomposition for reduced predictions] builds on this same separation. That is where we introduce projection of the fixed operator, load and output components into a reduced space.

*Superposition* follows from linearity of a solve with a *fixed* operator: if stem:[Gz_i=b_i], then stem:[G(\sum_i c_i z_i)=\sum_i c_i b_i]. The advanced xref:certification/residual-bound.adoc#efficient-residual[offline/online certificate] uses this property for fixed-metric Riesz representatives.
It does not imply stem:[A(\mu)^{-1}=\sum_q\Theta_q^a(\mu)A_q^{-1}]; that inverse formula is generally false.
