== States, spaces and parameters

[cols="1,3",options="header"]
|===
|Symbol |Meaning
|stem:[\mu\in\mathcal P] |Physical or geometric parameter; a parameter vector when necessary.
|stem:[V_h], stem:[n=\dim V_h] |Discrete full-order state space and its dimension.

|stem:[Z\in\mathbb R^{n\times r}] |Full-column-rank reduced basis matrix.

Use stem:[\mathbf a] for reduced coefficients and stem:[a_\mu(\cdot,\cdot)] for a bilinear form; make the distinction explicit.
A reduced model approximates the discrete full-order model. Its error relative to the physical system also involves discretization error and model discrepancy.

[[inner-products]]
== Inner products, projections and POD

Specify a symmetric positive definite Gram matrix stem:[G] before reporting a state norm:

[stem]
++++
\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf w\rangle_G=\mathbf v^T G\mathbf w,
\qquad \|\mathbf v\|_G=\sqrt{\mathbf v^TG\mathbf v}.
++++

A mass matrix may represent an stem:[L^2] inner product; another Gram matrix may represent an energy or stem:[H^1] inner product.
The Euclidean norm corresponds to stem:[G=I]. Do not substitute it for a physical norm without stating the change.
For a uniform one-dimensional grid, a discrete integral often contains a factor stem:[h]; use the same convention in errors, Riesz representatives and sensor stability calculations.

[[energy-norms]]
=== Two energy norms: fixed reference and current parameter

For a *symmetric coercive* bilinear form, the energy norm at the current parameter is

[stem]
++++
\lvert\!\lvert\!\lvert v\rvert\!\rvert\!\rvert_\mu
=\|v\|_{a_\mu}=\sqrt{a_\mu(v,v)}.
++++

Triple bars and the subscript stem:[a_\mu] are two notations for the *same norm*. When a discussion fixes stem:[\mu], we may omit the subscript on the triple bars; they still mean the current-parameter energy norm.

Choose a reference parameter stem:[\bar\mu] once and keep it fixed. The reference-energy convention used in the thermal practical is

[stem]
++++
(w,v)_V=a_{\bar\mu}(w,v),\qquad
\|v\|_V=\lvert\!\lvert\!\lvert v\rvert\!\rvert\!\rvert_{\bar\mu},
\qquad G=A(\bar\mu).
++++

Thus stem:[\|\mathbf v\|_G] is the coordinate version of stem:[\|v\|_V], whereas stem:[\sqrt{\mathbf v^TA(\mu)\mathbf v}] is the current-parameter energy norm. They agree at stem:[\mu=\bar\mu], but generally differ elsewhere. The notation stem:[\|\cdot\|_V] can also denote another declared fixed Hilbert norm in a different example; check the metric specified on that page.

Coercivity and continuity compare the norms:

[stem]
++++
\sqrt{\alpha(\mu)}\|v\|_V
\leq\lvert\!\lvert\!\lvert v\rvert\!\rvert\!\rvert_\mu
\leq\sqrt{\gamma(\mu)}\|v\|_V.
++++

The constants refer to this *same fixed norm*. A certified lower bound satisfies stem:[0<\alpha_{\mathrm{LB}}(\mu)\leq\alpha(\mu)]; it is not necessarily the exact coercivity constant. See xref:certification/coercivity-lower-bound.adoc[the min-Theta construction].

For the thermal model stem:[A(\mu)=\mu K+M], use stem:[\bar\mu=1] and stem:[G=K+M]. Snapshot weights stem:[w_j] in POD are separate: they weight sampled solutions, whereas stem:[G] measures each spatial vector.

All Cholesky formulas in these notes use the lower factor stem:[G=LL^T], as returned by `np.linalg.cholesky(G)`. State coordinates become stem:[L^T\mathbf v]; functional coordinates become stem:[L^{-1}\mathbf b]. A source using an upper factor stem:[G=C^TC] instead has stem:[C=L^T].

[[orthogonal-projection]]
[.prop,caption=""]
.Proposition — Projection in a specified metric
====
If stem:[Z] has full column rank and stem:[G] is symmetric positive definite, the best approximation to stem:[\mathbf u] in the range of stem:[Z], measured in stem:[\|\cdot\|_G], is

[stem]
++++
P_G\mathbf u=Z(Z^TGZ)^{-1}Z^TG\mathbf u.
++++

For a stem:[G]-orthonormal basis, stem:[Z^TGZ=I_r], this becomes stem:[P_G\mathbf u=ZZ^TG\mathbf u].
====

[%collapsible.proof,caption=""]
.Proof
====
Minimizing stem:[(\mathbf u-Z\mathbf a)^TG(\mathbf u-Z\mathbf a)] gives the normal equations stem:[Z^TGZ\mathbf a=Z^TG\mathbf u].
The matrix is positive definite because stem:[Z] has full column rank. The unique solution gives the stated projection.
====

Write stem:[S=[\mathbf u_1,\ldots,\mathbf u_m]] for the raw snapshot matrix and stem:[Z] for the retained basis. Normalizing or compressing snapshots changes their coordinates; truncating changes their span.
For POD, record the snapshot parameters, snapshot weights, state metric, centering choice and rank criterion.
An unweighted SVD of the snapshot matrix minimizes Euclidean snapshot error; a weighted POD must account for the chosen state metric and sampling weights.
Projection error on training snapshots is different from reduced-solve error on unseen parameters.

[[residuals]]
== Residuals and certification

For a discrete linear system stem:[A(\mu)\mathbf u=\mathbf f(\mu)], write stem:[e=u_h-u_r] for the reduction error. The residual *functional* is stem:[R_\mu(v)=f_\mu(v)-a_\mu(u_r,v)]; its coefficient vector is stem:[\boldsymbol\rho=\mathbf f-A\mathbf u_r] (also written stem:[\mathbf r] in some practicals).
It acts as stem:[R_\mu(v)=\boldsymbol\rho^T\mathbf v]. Its dual norm associated with the fixed metric stem:[G] is

[stem]
++++
\|R_\mu\|_{V_h'}=\|R_\mu\|_{G'}=\sqrt{\boldsymbol\rho^TG^{-1}\boldsymbol\rho}.
++++

The *energy-dual* norm instead uses the current energy norm in its denominator:

[stem]
++++
\|R_\mu\|_{a_\mu'}
=\sup_{v\ne0}\frac{|R_\mu(v)|}{\|v\|_{a_\mu}}
=\sqrt{\boldsymbol\rho^TA(\mu)^{-1}\boldsymbol\rho}.
++++

Here stem:[G'] labels the dual norm induced by stem:[G]; stem:[a_\mu'] labels the dual norm induced by stem:[a_\mu]. These are shorthand norm labels, not transposes or derivatives.
The energy-dual identity assumes symmetry and positive definiteness. It involves a different Riesz solve from the fixed-dual norm. Triple bars denote a *state* norm; do not apply them to a residual coefficient vector as a substitute for its dual norm.

The xref:foundations/riesz.adoc[Hilbert-space and Riesz representation notes] introduce the dual space, prove the norm identity and work through a non-diagonal matrix example.
Compute this with a Riesz solve, not an explicit matrix inverse.
The xref:certification/residual-bound.adoc#bounds-summary[bounds table] summarizes the assumptions and conclusions; efficient factored estimator evaluation is optional advanced reading.
A residual norm alone is not an error bound: state the stability assumptions and any certified lower bound used.
Write stem:[\Delta] for a verified upper bound on an explicitly named error. Its *effectivity* is that bound divided by the *same error quantity*: for example stem:[\Delta_G/\|e\|_V] for the fixed state norm, stem:[\Delta_a/\|e\|_{a_\mu}] for the energy norm, or stem:[\Delta_s/|s_h-s_r|] for an output. The denominator must be nonzero; exact zero-error cases do not have a defined ratio. A guaranteed upper bound has effectivity at least one in exact arithmetic.
A *compliant output* means stem:[\ell_\mu=f_\mu], in which case symmetry and Galerkin orthogonality give stem:[s_h-s_r=\|e\|_{a_\mu}^2]. An arbitrary mean temperature or sensor output need not be compliant.
Identify whether the bound concerns the discrete model, continuous PDE or an output.

[[observations]]
== Observations and sequential assimilation

[stem]
++++
\mathbf y_k=H_k\mathbf u_k+\boldsymbol\varepsilon_k,
\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_k)=R_k.
++++

Here stem:[m] is the number of observations, stem:[H_k\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}] is the linear observation operator, and stem:[R_k] is measurement-noise covariance.
State explicitly when observations are nonlinear or sensors are continuous functionals.
Use stem:[Q_k] for process-noise covariance and stem:[P_k^f,P_k^a] for forecast and analysis state-error covariances.
The superscripts stem:[f] and stem:[a] mean forecast and analysis; stem:[k] indexes time, while stem:[N_e] is ensemble size.

GEIM and PBDW chapters must specify both the background-space norm and the sensor functionals, including their normalization.
KF/EnKF chapters must specify forecast dynamics, observation operator, covariance assumptions and ensemble initialization.
Synthetic truth is available for evaluation in a teaching experiment; an assimilation method must not use that hidden state as an input.
