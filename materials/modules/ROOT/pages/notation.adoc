[[slide-correspondence]]
== The original slides are the reference

The course uses the original spaces stem:[X], stem:[X^{\mathcal N}] and stem:[X_N], and dimensions stem:[\mathcal N] and stem:[N]. We do not introduce a second family of space symbols when changing chapters.

[cols="1,3",options="header"]
|===
|Symbol |Meaning throughout the notes
|stem:[X] |Continuous state Hilbert space, with fixed inner product stem:[(\cdot,\cdot)_X].
|stem:[X^{\mathcal N}\subset X] |Full-order finite-element space; stem:[\mathcal N=\dim X^{\mathcal N}].
|stem:[X_N\subset X^{\mathcal N}] |Galerkin reduced/background space; stem:[N=\dim X_N].
|stem:[u^{\mathcal N}], stem:[u_N] |Full-order and reduced states; outputs stem:[s^{\mathcal N}], stem:[s_N].
|stem:[D_\mu] |Admissible parameter domain. The non-affine slides abbreviate the same domain as stem:[\mathcal D].
|stem:[a_\mu(w,v)=a(w,v;\mu)] |Two written forms of the same bilinear form; this page declares the shorthand once.
|stem:[Z\in\mathbb R^{\mathcal N\times N}] |Reduced basis *matrix*, whose columns represent functions in stem:[X_N].
|===

The older notes used stem:[V,V_h,V_r] and stem:[n,r] for the same spaces and dimensions. Those are legacy aliases, not extra mathematical objects. Executable Python retains `n` for stem:[\mathcal N] and `r` for stem:[N]; an array's actual shape states which dimension is being counted. This avoids silently changing working notebook interfaces.
The SVD stem:[S=U\Sigma V^T] retains its right-singular-vector matrix stem:[V]; it is not a state function space.
The symbol stem:[Z] occasionally denotes a generic vector space in the introductory PDF. In the course, the function space is stem:[X] and stem:[Z] always denotes a reduced basis matrix.

== States, spaces and parameters

[cols="1,3",options="header"]
|===
|Symbol |Meaning
|stem:[\mu\in D_\mu] |Physical or geometric parameter; a parameter vector when necessary.
|stem:[X^{\mathcal N}], stem:[\mathcal N=\dim X^{\mathcal N}] |Discrete full-order state space and its dimension.
|stem:[u^{\mathcal N}(\mu)], stem:[\mathbf u(\mu)\in\mathbb R^{\mathcal N}] |Discrete state and its coefficient vector in a specified full-order basis.
|stem:[X_N\subset X^{\mathcal N}], stem:[N\ll \mathcal N] |Reduced trial space and its dimension.
|stem:[Z\in\mathbb R^{\mathcal N\times N}] |Full-column-rank reduced basis matrix.
|stem:[\mathbf a(\mu)\in\mathbb R^N] |Reduced coefficients; reconstructed state stem:[\mathbf u_N=Z\mathbf a].
|stem:[s(\mu)=\ell(u^{\mathcal N}(\mu))] |Quantity of interest. A scalar output is distinct from the full state and from the observation vector.
|===

Use stem:[\mathbf a] for reduced coefficients and stem:[a_\mu(\cdot,\cdot)] for a bilinear form; make the distinction explicit.
A reduced model approximates the discrete full-order model. Its error relative to the physical system also involves discretization error and model discrepancy.

[[inner-products]]
== Inner products, projections and POD

Specify a symmetric positive definite Gram matrix stem:[G] before reporting a state norm:

[stem]
++++
\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf w\rangle_G=\mathbf v^T G\mathbf w,
\qquad \|\mathbf v\|_G=\sqrt{\mathbf v^TG\mathbf v}.
++++

A mass matrix may represent an stem:[L^2] inner product; another Gram matrix may represent an energy or stem:[H^1] inner product.
The Euclidean norm corresponds to stem:[G=I]. Do not substitute it for a physical norm without stating the change.
For a uniform one-dimensional grid, a discrete integral often contains a factor stem:[h]; use the same convention in errors, Riesz representatives and sensor stability calculations.

[[energy-norms]]
=== Two energy norms: fixed reference and current parameter

For a *symmetric coercive* bilinear form, the energy norm at the current parameter is

[stem]
++++
\lvert\!\lvert\!\lvert v\rvert\!\rvert\!\rvert_\mu
=\|v\|_{a_\mu}=\sqrt{a_\mu(v,v)}.
++++

Triple bars and the subscript stem:[a_\mu] are two notations for the *same norm*. When a discussion fixes stem:[\mu], we may omit the subscript on the triple bars; they still mean the current-parameter energy norm.

Choose a reference parameter stem:[\bar\mu] once and keep it fixed. The reference-energy convention used in the thermal practical is

[stem]
++++
(w,v)_X=a_{\bar\mu}(w,v),\qquad
\|v\|_X=\lvert\!\lvert\!\lvert v\rvert\!\rvert\!\rvert_{\bar\mu},
\qquad G=A(\bar\mu).
++++

Thus stem:[\|\mathbf v\|_G] is the coordinate version of stem:[\|v\|_X], whereas stem:[\sqrt{\mathbf v^TA(\mu)\mathbf v}] is the current-parameter energy norm. They agree at stem:[\mu=\bar\mu], but generally differ elsewhere. The notation stem:[\|\cdot\|_X] can also denote another declared fixed Hilbert norm in a different example; check the metric specified on that page.

Coercivity and continuity compare the norms:

[stem]
++++
\sqrt{\alpha(\mu)}\|v\|_X
\leq\lvert\!\lvert\!\lvert v\rvert\!\rvert\!\rvert_\mu
\leq\sqrt{\gamma(\mu)}\|v\|_X.
++++

The constants refer to this *same fixed norm*. A certified lower bound satisfies stem:[0<\alpha_{\mathrm{LB}}(\mu)\leq\alpha(\mu)]; it is not necessarily the exact coercivity constant. See xref:certification/coercivity-lower-bound.adoc[the min-Theta construction].

For the thermal model stem:[A(\mu)=\mu K+M], use stem:[\bar\mu=1] and stem:[G=K+M]. Snapshot weights stem:[w_j] in POD are separate: they weight sampled solutions, whereas stem:[G] measures each spatial vector.

All Cholesky formulas in these notes use the lower factor stem:[G=LL^T], as returned by `np.linalg.cholesky(G)`. State coordinates become stem:[L^T\mathbf v]; functional coordinates become stem:[L^{-1}\mathbf b]. A source using an upper factor stem:[G=C^TC] instead has stem:[C=L^T].

[[orthogonal-projection]]
[.prop,caption=""]
.Proposition — Projection in a specified metric
====
If stem:[Z] has full column rank and stem:[G] is symmetric positive definite, the best approximation to stem:[\mathbf u] in the range of stem:[Z], measured in stem:[\|\cdot\|_G], is

[stem]
++++
P_G\mathbf u=Z(Z^TGZ)^{-1}Z^TG\mathbf u.
++++

For a stem:[G]-orthonormal basis, stem:[Z^TGZ=I_N], this becomes stem:[P_G\mathbf u=ZZ^TG\mathbf u].
====

[%collapsible.proof,caption=""]
.Proof
====
Minimizing stem:[(\mathbf u-Z\mathbf a)^TG(\mathbf u-Z\mathbf a)] gives the normal equations stem:[Z^TGZ\mathbf a=Z^TG\mathbf u].
The matrix is positive definite because stem:[Z] has full column rank. The unique solution gives the stated projection.
====

Write stem:[S=[\mathbf u_1,\ldots,\mathbf u_m]] for the raw snapshot matrix and stem:[Z] for the retained basis. Normalizing or compressing snapshots changes their coordinates; truncating changes their span.
For POD, record the snapshot parameters, snapshot weights, state metric, centering choice and rank criterion.
An unweighted SVD of the snapshot matrix minimizes Euclidean snapshot error; a weighted POD must account for the chosen state metric and sampling weights.
Projection error on training snapshots is different from reduced-solve error on unseen parameters.

[[residuals]]
== Residuals and certification

For a discrete linear system stem:[A(\mu)\mathbf u=\mathbf f(\mu)], write stem:[e=u^{\mathcal N}-u_N] for the reduction error. The residual *functional* is stem:[R_\mu(v)=f_\mu(v)-a_\mu(u_N,v)]; its coefficient vector is stem:[\mathbf r_\mu=\mathbf f-A\mathbf u_N] (called `rho` or `r` in legacy numerical cells).
It acts as stem:[R_\mu(v)=\mathbf r_\mu^T\mathbf v]. Its dual norm associated with the fixed metric stem:[G] is

[stem]
++++
\|R_\mu\|_{(X^{\mathcal N})'}=\|R_\mu\|_{G'}=\sqrt{\mathbf r_\mu^TG^{-1}\mathbf r_\mu}.
++++

The *energy-dual* norm instead uses the current energy norm in its denominator:

[stem]
++++
\|R_\mu\|_{a_\mu'}
=\sup_{v\ne0}\frac{|R_\mu(v)|}{\|v\|_{a_\mu}}
=\sqrt{\mathbf r_\mu^TA(\mu)^{-1}\mathbf r_\mu}.
++++

Here stem:[G'] labels the dual norm induced by stem:[G]; stem:[a_\mu'] labels the dual norm induced by stem:[a_\mu]. These are shorthand norm labels, not transposes or derivatives.
The energy-dual identity assumes symmetry and positive definiteness. It involves a different Riesz solve from the fixed-dual norm. Triple bars denote a *state* norm; do not apply them to a residual coefficient vector as a substitute for its dual norm.

The xref:foundations/riesz.adoc[Hilbert-space and Riesz representation notes] introduce the dual space, prove the norm identity and work through a non-diagonal matrix example.
Compute this with a Riesz solve, not an explicit matrix inverse.
The xref:certification/residual-bound.adoc#bounds-summary[bounds table] summarizes the assumptions and conclusions; efficient factored estimator evaluation is optional advanced reading.
A residual norm alone is not an error bound: state the stability assumptions and any certified lower bound used.
Write stem:[\Delta] for a verified upper bound on an explicitly named error. Its *effectivity* is that bound divided by the *same error quantity*: for example stem:[\Delta_G/\|e\|_X] for the fixed state norm, stem:[\Delta_a/\|e\|_{a_\mu}] for the energy norm, or stem:[\Delta_s/|s^{\mathcal N}-s_N|] for an output. The denominator must be nonzero; exact zero-error cases do not have a defined ratio. A guaranteed upper bound has effectivity at least one in exact arithmetic.
A *compliant output* means stem:[\ell_\mu=f_\mu], in which case symmetry and Galerkin orthogonality give stem:[s^{\mathcal N}-s_N=\|e\|_{a_\mu}^2]. An arbitrary mean temperature or sensor output need not be compliant.
Identify whether the bound concerns the discrete model, continuous PDE or an output.

[[interpolation-and-measurements]]
== EIM, DEIM and GEIM: one interpolation vocabulary

The source of these conventions is xref:course-rom::attachment$lecture-rbobm-beamer-non-affine.pdf[the original EIM slides]. The GEIM extension retains their basis functions and coefficients; only the measurement operator changes.

[cols="1,3",options="header"]
|===
|Symbol |Meaning
|stem:[g(x;\mu)] |The spatial term being interpolated. It is not automatically the PDE solution stem:[u^{\mathcal N}].
|stem:[\xi_j] |Generating function: the selected raw snapshot before subtracting its interpolant.
|stem:[r_j] |Interpolation residual of stem:[\xi_j]; distinct from the PDE residual functional stem:[R_\mu].
|stem:[\rho_j] |Normalized interpolation basis function. It is neither a PDE residual nor a sensor Riesz representative.
|stem:[\gamma_j(\mu)] or stem:[\gamma_j(y)] |Interpolation coefficients, recovered from sampled source values or measurement data.
|stem:[I_M^x g] |EIM point interpolant at the spatial magic points stem:[T_M=\{t^1,\ldots,t^M\}]. These stem:[t^i] are not time instants.
|stem:[L_i\in(X^{\mathcal N})'] |Bounded linear measurement functional in GEIM. The scalar quantity of interest is stem:[\ell], a different object.
|stem:[I_M^L u] |GEIM interpolant satisfying stem:[L_i(I_M^L u)=L_i(u)].
|stem:[\mathbb B_M] |Interpolation matrix: stem:[(\mathbb B_M)_{ij}=\rho_j(t^i)] for EIM, stem:[(\mathbb B_M)_{ij}=L_i(\rho_j)] for GEIM.
|stem:[\mathcal R_M] |Full-grid array of interpolation functions stem:[\rho_j], called `rho` in the EIM/GEIM notebooks; distinct from the small matrix stem:[\mathbb B_M].
|stem:[U_M] |Fixed POD basis of the term in DEIM; its sampled matrix need not be unit triangular.
|stem:[H_M], stem:[y] |Selected measurement matrix and data: stem:[y_i=L_i(u)+\varepsilon_i].
|stem:[W] |Sensor Riesz matrix in PBDW: stem:[GW=H^T]. Columns stem:[w_i] represent sensors in the state metric, with stem:[Gw_i=h_i]; they are not the GEIM interpolation basis.
|===

The EIM approximation space stem:[X_M=\operatorname{span}\{\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_M\}] is the space of the interpolated *term*. The state prediction space is stem:[X_N]. In GEIM we write stem:[X_M^{\mathrm{GEIM}}] when both spaces occur in the same discussion.
The selected parameter set stem:[S_M=\{\mu^1_{\mathrm{EIM}},\ldots,\mu^M_{\mathrm{EIM}}\}] is not the Python snapshot array `S`.
The source-grid size stem:[N_q] is distinct from the FE dimension stem:[\mathcal N] and from the reduced-state dimension stem:[N].
The interpolation coefficients stem:[\gamma_j] are not the continuity constant stem:[\gamma(\mu)]; the subscript identifies their role.

== Affine coefficient families

Use stem:[\Theta_q^a(\mu)], stem:[\Theta_q^f(\mu)] and stem:[\Theta_q^\ell(\mu)] for the affine operator, load and output coefficients. Their term counts are stem:[Q_a,Q_f,Q_\ell]. EIM recovers an approximate affine coefficient family through stem:[\gamma_j(\mu)]. These are scalars; their parameter-independent forms or matrices are declared separately.

== Quantities whose meaning must be declared locally

* stem:[G] is a fixed state metric; stem:[M] in the heat equation is a mass matrix, while an integer stem:[M] in EIM/GEIM counts interpolation functions or sensors. State the object and dimensions at the start of the example.
* The stiffness matrix stem:[K], Kalman gain stem:[K_k], time-step count stem:[K] and ensemble size stem:[N_e] have different declared types.
* stem:[L] in stem:[G=LL^T] is a Cholesky matrix. The indexed stem:[L_i] are measurement functionals.
* stem:[P_k] and stem:[Q_k] below are covariance matrices, not interpolation or reduced-basis matrices. Matrix selection operators always carry their defining equation.
* POD spatial metric stem:[G] and snapshot weights stem:[w_j] are separate. Equal time weights are stem:[w_k=1/K] when the objective is a mean square over stem:[K] time snapshots.

[[observations]]
== Observations and sequential assimilation

[stem]
++++
\mathbf y_k=H_k\mathbf u_k+\boldsymbol\varepsilon_k,
\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_k)=R_k.
++++

Here stem:[m] is the number of observations, stem:[H_k\in\mathbb R^{m\times \mathcal N}] is the linear observation operator, and stem:[R_k] is measurement-noise covariance.
State explicitly when observations are nonlinear or sensors are continuous functionals.
Use stem:[Q_k] for process-noise covariance and stem:[P_k^f,P_k^a] for forecast and analysis state-error covariances.
The superscripts stem:[f] and stem:[a] mean forecast and analysis; stem:[k] indexes time, while stem:[N_e] is ensemble size.

GEIM and PBDW chapters must specify both the background-space norm and the sensor functionals, including their normalization.
KF/EnKF chapters must specify forecast dynamics, observation operator, covariance assumptions and ensemble initialization.
Synthetic truth is available for evaluation in a teaching experiment; an assimilation method must not use that hidden state as an input.
