Hilbert spaces, dual norms and Riesz representation

A residual or a sensor is a rule acting on a state. A state norm measures vectors; a dual norm measures these rules. This page explains the connection needed to compute residual bounds and to construct data-assimilation updates.

Course route: read through the matrix example before session 3. The function-space example and the existence proof are supplementary reading. The efficient offline/online implementation of a certificate is separate advanced reading.

From inner products to linear functionals

A real inner-product space has a symmetric bilinear product \((u,v)_V\) that is positive on every nonzero vector. It defines the norm \(\|v\|_V=\sqrt{(v,v)_V}\). A Hilbert space is complete in this norm: every Cauchy sequence converges to an element of the space. Finite-dimensional spaces with positive definite inner products are Hilbert spaces. Completeness matters for infinite-dimensional spaces of functions, where a limit must remain admissible.

A linear functional maps a vector to a scalar and respects linear combinations. It is bounded if a constant \(C\) exists such that \(|\ell(v)|\leq C\|v\|_V\) for every \(v\). For a linear map, this is equivalent to continuity. The dual space \(V'\) consists of all bounded linear functionals, with norm

\[\|\ell\|_{V'}=\sup_{v\ne0}\frac{|\ell(v)|}{\|v\|_V} =\sup_{\|v\|_V=1}|\ell(v)|.\]

Thus the dual norm is the largest response to a unit-norm test direction, not the response to one arbitrarily chosen vector. Rescaling a nonzero test vector does not change the quotient. In finite dimensions every linear functional is bounded, but its norm still depends on the chosen state metric.

Riesz representation: turn a functional into a vector

Riesz representation theorem (real Hilbert space)

For each \(\ell\in V'\), there is exactly one \(z_\ell\in V\) such that

\[\ell(v)=(z_\ell,v)_V\quad\hbox{for every }v\in V, \qquad \|\ell\|_{V'}=\|z_\ell\|_V.\]

The vector \(z_\ell\) is the Riesz representative (also called the Riesz representer) of the functional in this inner product. The Riesz map sends a vector to its inner-product functional; its inverse sends the functional to its representative.

Why the norm identity and uniqueness hold

By Cauchy–Schwarz, \(|\ell(v)|=|(z_\ell,v)_V|\leq\|z_\ell\|_V\|v\|_V\). For nonzero \(z_\ell\), choose \(v=z_\ell/\|z_\ell\|_V\) to attain equality in the supremum. If the representative is zero, both norms vanish. If two representatives exist, their difference has zero inner product with every vector; testing with that difference proves uniqueness.

The theorem replaces a supremum over all directions by one vector computation. The representative depends on the inner product: changing the norm changes the Riesz map, even when the functional itself is unchanged.

Computing a representative in a discrete basis

Let \(\{\phi_i\}_{i=1}^n\) be a basis of \(V_h\). Define the Gram matrix and the functional coefficients by

\[G_{ij}=(\phi_i,\phi_j)_V,\qquad b_i=\ell(\phi_i).\]

For \(v=\sum_i v_i\phi_i\), linearity gives \(\ell(v)=b^Tv\). If the representative has coordinate vector \(z\), then its inner product with this test function is \(z^TGv\). Equality for every coordinate vector therefore gives

\[Gz=b,\qquad \|\ell\|_{V_h'}^2=z^TGz=b^Tz=b^TG^{-1}b.\]

The functional coefficients \(b\) are generally not the state coefficients \(z\). They coincide only in an orthonormal basis for the declared metric. Solve a linear system; the displayed inverse describes the mathematics, not the preferred numerical operation.

Worked example: a non-Euclidean metric

Take

\[G=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix},\qquad \ell(v)=v_1,\qquad b=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}.\]

Solving the two equations gives

\[z=\begin{bmatrix}2/3\\-1/3\end{bmatrix},\qquad \|\ell\|_{V_h'}=\sqrt{2/3}.\]

The Euclidean norm of \(b\) is one, so using it would give the wrong dual norm. The unit test direction \(v_*=z/\sqrt{2/3}\) attains the supremum: its state norm is one and its functional value is \(\sqrt{2/3}\).

import numpy as np

G = np.array([[2., 1.], [1., 2.]])
b = np.array([1., 0.])
z = np.linalg.solve(G, b)
dual_norm = np.sqrt(b @ z)
v_star = z / dual_norm
print(z, dual_norm)  # [ 0.66666667 -0.33333333], 0.81649658...
assert np.isclose(v_star @ G @ v_star, 1.)
assert np.isclose(b @ v_star, dual_norm)

One metric, two coordinate transformations

A Cholesky factor written as \(G=L^TL\) maps state coordinates to Euclidean coordinates:

\[\|v\|_G=\|Lv\|_2.\]

A functional instead acts as \(b^Tv=(L^{-T}b)^T(Lv)\), so its dual coordinates transform differently:

\[\|\ell\|_{V_h'}=\|L^{-T}b\|_2.\]

This explains both the weighting of snapshots in POD and the inverse metric in residual and sensor calculations. NumPy’s lower Cholesky factor satisfies \(G=CC^T\), so use \(L=C^T\). Compute dual coordinates with np.linalg.solve(L.T, b); do not multiply them by the state-norm factor.

Which error is being measured?

A functional on a continuous Hilbert space can be restricted to a finite-dimensional subspace \(V_h\). Its discrete representative is the orthogonal projection of its continuous representative onto \(V_h\): both have the same inner products with every test function in that subspace. Consequently,

\[\|\ell|_{V_h}\|_{V_h'}\leq\|\ell\|_{V'}.\]

A residual norm computed only with full-order discrete tests bounds reduction error relative to the discrete solution, under the stated stability assumption. It does not automatically bound the error relative to the continuous PDE solution. Restricting further to the reduced Galerkin test space makes its residual identically zero there; that zero does not certify the full-order error.

Supplementary reading: function-space examples

In \(V=L^2(\Omega)\) with its integral inner product, the functional \(\ell(v)=\int_\Omega g v\) for \(g\in L^2(\Omega)\) has representative \(g\). Cauchy–Schwarz proves boundedness and its dual norm is \(\|g\|_{L^2}\). A normalized average on a region \(D\) of positive measure uses \(g=\mathbf1_D/|D|\), with norm \(1/\sqrt{|D|}\). Smaller averaging regions have larger norms in this metric.

Point evaluation is not a bounded functional on \(L^2\): changing a function at one point does not even change its equivalence class. A continuous sensor model must specify a space where its measurements make sense. Discrete point sensors are well defined, but their norms can grow as the mesh is refined; for \(G=hI\), a coordinate sensor has norm \(1/\sqrt h\).

Now use \(V=H_0^1(0,1)\) with inner product \((z,v)_V=\int_0^1 z'v'\). This Sobolev space consists of square-integrable functions with square-integrable weak derivative and zero endpoint trace. Poincaré’s inequality makes the derivative norm a Hilbert-space norm here. For the load \(\ell(v)=\int_0^1 gv\), its Riesz representative solves the weak problem

\[\int_0^1 z'v'=\int_0^1 gv\quad\text{for every }v\in H_0^1(0,1).\]

For sufficiently regular \(g\), integration by parts identifies \(-z''=g\) with zero boundary values. For \(g=1\), the solution is \(z(x)=x(1-x)/2\), and its derivative energy is \(\int_0^1|z'|^2=1/12\). Thus the load’s dual norm is \(1/\sqrt{12}\). The Riesz solve is determined by the chosen inner product; it need not use the parameter-dependent PDE operator.

Supplementary reading: why a representative exists

For a nonzero bounded functional, its kernel \(N=\ker\ell\) is a closed subspace. The Hilbert-space projection theorem states that every vector has a unique decomposition into a vector in a closed subspace and a vector orthogonal to it. Choose \(w\) with \(\ell(w)\ne0\) and let \(q=w-P_Nw\). Then \(q\ne0\), it is orthogonal to the kernel, and \(\ell(q)=\ell(w)\). For every \(v\), the vector below belongs to the kernel:

\[v-\frac{\ell(v)}{\ell(q)}q\in N.\]

Taking its inner product with \(q\) gives

\[(q,v)_V=\frac{\ell(v)}{\ell(q)}\|q\|_V^2, \qquad z_\ell=\frac{\ell(q)}{\|q\|_V^2}q.\]

This constructs the representative. The zero functional has representative zero. The projection theorem is the functional-analysis ingredient used in this existence proof; the matrix construction above needs only positive definiteness of \(G\).

Practice and connections

  2. Compute the representative of a sensor row \(h_s^T\). Verify \(h_s^Tv=z_s^TGv\) for several vectors.

  3. Explain why a residual that vanishes on \(V_r\) can still have a nonzero representative in \(V_h\).
