Notation and mathematical conventions

These conventions connect the reduction and assimilation parts of the library. Start here when a symbol in a proof or a notebook is unfamiliar; use the mathematical reminders for the definitions behind the symbols. The M2 course selects a short route through these topics.

This page and the reminders adapt the notation and ingredients slides (PDF), especially the projection, notation, problem-statement and norm slides (PDF pages 27–34). They are authored AsciiDoc notes, not an automatic PDF conversion.

Use \(\mathbf a\) for reduced coefficients and \(a_\mu(\cdot,\cdot)\) for a bilinear form; make the distinction explicit. A reduced model approximates the discrete full-order model. Its error relative to the physical system also involves discretization error and model discrepancy.

Inner products, projections and POD

Specify a symmetric positive definite Gram matrix \(G\) before reporting a state norm:

\[\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf w\rangle_G=\mathbf v^T G\mathbf w, \qquad \|\mathbf v\|_G=\sqrt{\mathbf v^TG\mathbf v}.\]

A mass matrix may represent an \(L^2\) inner product; another Gram matrix may represent an energy or \(H^1\) inner product. The Euclidean norm corresponds to \(G=I\). Do not substitute it for a physical norm without stating the change. For a uniform one-dimensional grid, a discrete integral often contains a factor \(h\); use the same convention in errors, Riesz representatives and sensor stability calculations.

Two energy norms: fixed reference and current parameter

For a symmetric coercive bilinear form, the energy norm at the current parameter is

\[\lvert\!\lvert\!\lvert v\rvert\!\rvert\!\rvert_\mu =\|v\|_{a_\mu}=\sqrt{a_\mu(v,v)}.\]

Triple bars and the subscript \(a_\mu\) are two notations for the same norm. When a discussion fixes \(\mu\), we may omit the subscript on the triple bars; they still mean the current-parameter energy norm.

Choose a reference parameter \(\bar\mu\) once and keep it fixed. The reference-energy convention used in the thermal practical is

\[(w,v)_V=a_{\bar\mu}(w,v),\qquad \|v\|_V=\lvert\!\lvert\!\lvert v\rvert\!\rvert\!\rvert_{\bar\mu}, \qquad G=A(\bar\mu).\]

Thus \(\|\mathbf v\|_G\) is the coordinate version of \(\|v\|_V\), whereas \(\sqrt{\mathbf v^TA(\mu)\mathbf v}\) is the current-parameter energy norm. They agree at \(\mu=\bar\mu\), but generally differ elsewhere. The notation \(\|\cdot\|_V\) can also denote another declared fixed Hilbert norm in a different example; check the metric specified on that page.

Coercivity and continuity compare the norms:

\[\sqrt{\alpha(\mu)}\|v\|_V \leq\lvert\!\lvert\!\lvert v\rvert\!\rvert\!\rvert_\mu \leq\sqrt{\gamma(\mu)}\|v\|_V.\]

The constants refer to this same fixed norm. A certified lower bound satisfies \(0<\alpha_{\mathrm{LB}}(\mu)\leq\alpha(\mu)\); it is not necessarily the exact coercivity constant. See the min-Theta construction.

For the thermal model \(A(\mu)=\mu K+M\), use \(\bar\mu=1\) and \(G=K+M\). Snapshot weights \(w_j\) in POD are separate: they weight sampled solutions, whereas \(G\) measures each spatial vector.

All Cholesky formulas in these notes use the lower factor \(G=LL^T\), as returned by np.linalg.cholesky(G). State coordinates become \(L^T\mathbf v\); functional coordinates become \(L^{-1}\mathbf b\). A source using an upper factor \(G=C^TC\) instead has \(C=L^T\).

Proposition — Projection in a specified metric

If \(Z\) has full column rank and \(G\) is symmetric positive definite, the best approximation to \(\mathbf u\) in the range of \(Z\), measured in \(\|\cdot\|_G\), is

\[P_G\mathbf u=Z(Z^TGZ)^{-1}Z^TG\mathbf u.\]

For a \(G\)-orthonormal basis, \(Z^TGZ=I_r\), this becomes \(P_G\mathbf u=ZZ^TG\mathbf u\).

Proof

Minimizing \((\mathbf u-Z\mathbf a)^TG(\mathbf u-Z\mathbf a)\) gives the normal equations \(Z^TGZ\mathbf a=Z^TG\mathbf u\). The matrix is positive definite because \(Z\) has full column rank. The unique solution gives the stated projection.

Write \(S=[\mathbf u_1,\ldots,\mathbf u_m\)] for the raw snapshot matrix and \(Z\) for the retained basis. Normalizing or compressing snapshots changes their coordinates; truncating changes their span. For POD, record the snapshot parameters, snapshot weights, state metric, centering choice and rank criterion. An unweighted SVD of the snapshot matrix minimizes Euclidean snapshot error; a weighted POD must account for the chosen state metric and sampling weights. Projection error on training snapshots is different from reduced-solve error on unseen parameters.

Residuals and certification

For a discrete linear system \(A(\mu)\mathbf u=\mathbf f(\mu)\), write \(e=u_h-u_r\) for the reduction error. The residual functional is \(R_\mu(v)=f_\mu(v)-a_\mu(u_r,v)\); its coefficient vector is \(\boldsymbol\rho=\mathbf f-A\mathbf u_r\) (also written \(\mathbf r\) in some practicals). It acts as \(R_\mu(v)=\boldsymbol\rho^T\mathbf v\). Its dual norm associated with the fixed metric \(G\) is

\[\|R_\mu\|_{V_h'}=\|R_\mu\|_{G'}=\sqrt{\boldsymbol\rho^TG^{-1}\boldsymbol\rho}.\]

The energy-dual norm instead uses the current energy norm in its denominator:

\[\|R_\mu\|_{a_\mu'} =\sup_{v\ne0}\frac{|R_\mu(v)|}{\|v\|_{a_\mu}} =\sqrt{\boldsymbol\rho^TA(\mu)^{-1}\boldsymbol\rho}.\]

Here \(G'\) labels the dual norm induced by \(G\); \(a_\mu'\) labels the dual norm induced by \(a_\mu\). These are shorthand norm labels, not transposes or derivatives. The energy-dual identity assumes symmetry and positive definiteness. It involves a different Riesz solve from the fixed-dual norm. Triple bars denote a state norm; do not apply them to a residual coefficient vector as a substitute for its dual norm.

The Hilbert-space and Riesz representation notes introduce the dual space, prove the norm identity and work through a non-diagonal matrix example. Compute this with a Riesz solve, not an explicit matrix inverse. The bounds table summarizes the assumptions and conclusions; efficient factored estimator evaluation is optional advanced reading. A residual norm alone is not an error bound: state the stability assumptions and any certified lower bound used. Write \(\Delta\) for a verified upper bound on an explicitly named error. Its effectivity is that bound divided by the same error quantity: for example \(\Delta_G/\|e\|_V\) for the fixed state norm, \(\Delta_a/\|e\|_{a_\mu}\) for the energy norm, or \(\Delta_s/|s_h-s_r|\) for an output. The denominator must be nonzero; exact zero-error cases do not have a defined ratio. A guaranteed upper bound has effectivity at least one in exact arithmetic. A compliant output means \(\ell_\mu=f_\mu\), in which case symmetry and Galerkin orthogonality give \(s_h-s_r=\|e\|_{a_\mu}^2\). An arbitrary mean temperature or sensor output need not be compliant. Identify whether the bound concerns the discrete model, continuous PDE or an output.

Observations and sequential assimilation

\[\mathbf y_k=H_k\mathbf u_k+\boldsymbol\varepsilon_k, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_k)=R_k.\]

Here \(m\) is the number of observations, \(H_k\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\) is the linear observation operator, and \(R_k\) is measurement-noise covariance. State explicitly when observations are nonlinear or sensors are continuous functionals. Use \(Q_k\) for process-noise covariance and \(P_k^f,P_k^a\) for forecast and analysis state-error covariances. The superscripts \(f\) and \(a\) mean forecast and analysis; \(k\) indexes time, while \(N_e\) is ensemble size.

GEIM and PBDW chapters must specify both the background-space norm and the sensor functionals, including their normalization. KF/EnKF chapters must specify forecast dynamics, observation operator, covariance assumptions and ensemble initialization. Synthetic truth is available for evaluation in a teaching experiment; an assimilation method must not use that hidden state as an input.
