Ensemble Kalman filtering and reduced forecasts

Read the Kalman filter first. The EnKF propagates an ensemble through a possibly nonlinear model and estimates forecast covariances from its anomalies. It is not an exact nonlinear Bayesian filter; even an infinite ensemble need not make a non-Gaussian posterior Gaussian.

Forecast and sample covariance

Propagate each analysis member, adding independent process noise when that is part of the specified model. For \(N_e>1\) members, let \(X\) contain forecast states and \(Y\) their predicted observations. Subtract the respective ensemble means to obtain anomaly matrices.

\[\begin{aligned} C_{xy}&=\frac{X'Y'^T}{N_e-1}, & C_{yy}&=\frac{Y'Y'^T}{N_e-1},\\ K&=C_{xy}(C_{yy}+R)^{-1}. \end{aligned}\]

For linear observations, predicted observations are simply \(HX\). The anomaly matrix \(X'\in\mathbb R^{n\times N_e}\) has columns \(x_i-\bar x\) and satisfies \(X'\mathbf1=0\). Thus its rank, and the rank of its sample covariance, cannot exceed \(N_e-1\). The denominator stems from estimating the mean using those same samples: for independent samples with covariance \(P\),

\[\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(x_i-\bar x)^T\right] =N_eP-N_e\operatorname{Cov}(\bar x)=(N_e-1)P.\]

This explains the usual unbiased sample-covariance normalization. Filter members become dependent through their shared gain and observations; the sample formula does not make the resulting filter exact. Small ensembles can produce spurious distant correlations and underestimate uncertainty.

Stochastic analysis

\[x_i^+=x_i^-+K\bigl(y+\varepsilon_i-h(x_i^-)\bigr), \qquad\varepsilon_i\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}\sim\mathcal N(0,R).\]

The perturbations are independent of forecast errors. For linear observations and a fixed forecast ensemble, the gain is fixed before drawing perturbations. Let \(E'\) be the matrix of centered observation perturbations. Centering the member updates gives

\[X^{\prime+}=(I-KH)X^{\prime-}+KE'.\]

Conditionally on the forecast ensemble, the perturbations have mean zero, the mixed terms have zero expectation, and their sample covariance has expectation \(R\). Consequently the expected analysis sample covariance is

\[\mathbb E_\varepsilon[P_e^+\mid X^-] =(I-KH)P_e^-(I-KH)^T+KRK^T.\]

This recovers the observation-noise term of the Joseph formula in expectation. Without perturbations the second term is missing. In the scalar example, forecast variance 2 and gain \(2/3\) would leave variance \(2/9\) without perturbations, compared with the target \(2/3\). The missing \(4/9\) is exactly \(KRK^T\). A finite ensemble also has a random perturbation mean, of covariance \(R/N_e\); this adds sampling variability to the analysis mean. For nonlinear observations, the sample cross-covariance update remains an approximation, not the exact Gaussian conditioning formula for a linear observation. Deterministic square-root EnKFs use a different anomaly transform and are outside this course’s required implementation.

Multiplicative inflation scales anomalies about their mean; it must not scale the mean state. For anomaly multiplier \(\lambda\), use

\[x_i\leftarrow\bar x+\lambda(x_i-\bar x),\qquad P_e\leftarrow\lambda^2P_e.\]

To multiply covariance by a specified factor, multiply anomalies by its square root. Localization modifies sample correlations using additional spatial assumptions; it is not just increasing the ensemble size. Tune such choices with separate validation data and retain an untouched test experiment.

Algorithm — One stochastic EnKF cycle

The model propagates every member before sample covariances are formed. Use independent process-noise draws according to the stated forecast model and independent observation perturbations with covariance \(R\). The following cycle omits optional inflation and localization so that the basic stochastic update is explicit.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{One stochastic ensemble Kalman step}
\begin{algorithmic}
\INPUT Analysis ensemble $\{x_i^+\}_{i=1}^{N_e}$; forecast map $\mathcal F$; observation map $h$; data $y$; covariance $R$
\REQUIRE $N_e>1$ and a declared process-noise model
\FOR{$i=1$ \TO $N_e$}
  \STATE Draw independent process noise $w_i$
  \STATE $x_i^-\leftarrow\mathcal F(x_i^+)+w_i$
  \STATE $y_i^-\leftarrow h(x_i^-)$
\ENDFOR
\STATE Assemble $X=[x_i^-]$, $Y=[y_i^-]$ and their column means $\bar x,\bar y$
\STATE $X'\leftarrow X-\bar x\mathbf1^T$; $Y'\leftarrow Y-\bar y\mathbf1^T$
\STATE $C_{xy}\leftarrow X'Y'^T/(N_e-1)$; $C_{yy}\leftarrow Y'Y'^T/(N_e-1)$
\STATE Solve $(C_{yy}+R)K^T=C_{xy}^T$
\FOR{$i=1$ \TO $N_e$}
  \STATE Draw $\varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,R)$ independently
  \STATE $x_i^+\leftarrow x_i^-+K(y+\varepsilon_i-y_i^-)$
\ENDFOR
\OUTPUT Analysis ensemble and its sample mean/covariance
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For a deterministic forecast without modeled process noise, set every process-noise draw to zero. For a coefficient-space ROM filter, replace the state by its coefficient vector and use the reduced forecast and observation operators derived next.

Coupling to a reduced model

Start from the semi-discrete evolution problem, with fixed basis \(Z\):

\[M\dot u+A(\mu)u=f g(t),\qquad u_r(t)=Za(t).\]

Substitute the reduced state and test with \(Z\). Since the basis is time independent, its derivative is \(Z\dot a\). This yields

\[M_r\dot a+A_r(\mu)a=f_r g(t),\qquad M_r=Z^TMZ,\quad A_r=Z^TAZ,\quad f_r=Z^Tf.\]

Implicit Euler replaces the derivative by the backward difference and evaluates the stiffness term and forcing at the new time:

\[(M_r+\Delta t A_r)a_{k+1}=M_ra_k+\Delta t f_r g_{k+1}, \qquad M_r=Z^TMZ,\quad A_r=Z^TAZ.\]

If \(A(\mu)=\sum_q\theta_q(\mu)A_q\), store \(Z^TA_qZ\), \(M_r\) and \(f_r\) offline exactly as in affine prediction. Online, combine only reduced stiffness terms. For fixed parameter and time step, factor the reduced time-stepping matrix once and reuse its factors for every ensemble member and time step. If the parameter changes, update that small matrix and its factorization.

Substituting \(u_r=Za\) in the sensor equation gives \(y=HZ a+v\). Precompute the observation operator \(H_r=HZ\) and reduced scalar outputs. Filtering the coefficients costs much less than forming full-state covariances, but all ensemble corrections remain in the chosen reduced span. Inflation cannot create a direction absent from that span. Account separately for reduction error, parameter/model mismatch, observation noise and finite-ensemble variability. A small spread may reflect a restrictive subspace rather than reliable predictions.
