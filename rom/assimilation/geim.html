GEIM: interpolation from linear measurements

Read EIM first. We keep its generating functions \(\xi_j\), interpolation residuals \(r_j\), basis functions \(\rho_j\), coefficients \(\gamma_j\) and interpolation matrix \(\mathbb B_M\). Following the original slides, GEIM replaces the point evaluations \(\delta_{t^i}(v)=v(t^i)\) by bounded linear measurement functionals \(L_i(v)\). The scalar quantity of interest remains \(\ell(v)\); it is not the list of sensor readings. See the common notation for the complete correspondence.

From EIM to GEIM: change the measurements, keep the interpolation principle

\(g(t^i;\mu)\)

\(\mathbb B_M\gamma(\mu)=\mathbf g_M(\mu)\)

\(\mathbb B_M\gamma(y)=y\)

The generalized interpolant is

\[I_M^L u=\sum_{j=1}^M\gamma_j(y)\rho_j,\qquad L_i(I_M^L u)=L_i(u),\quad i=1,\ldots,M.\]

The superscript \(L\) identifies generalized measurements, while \(x\) in \(I_M^x\) identifies the point interpolation of EIM. The construction is otherwise the same kind of hierarchical interpolation. In EIM the parameter is supplied so that the selected function values can be evaluated. In GEIM the parameter or full state may be unknown: the online inputs are the measured values \(y\), not the hidden true field. This is state reconstruction, not identification of a conductivity parameter by nonlinear least squares.

Declare and normalize the sensor dictionary

Fix the state inner product \((\cdot,\cdot)_X\) and a dictionary \(\Sigma_L\subset(X^{\mathcal N})'\) of candidate sensors. Examples are localized averages and weighted integrals. For every candidate, specify its units and normalization before comparing responses. The practical uses normalized averages: every sensor returns one on the constant unit state. This physical normalization need not be unit normalization in the dual norm.

In coordinates, define a sensor by its coefficient vector \(h_s\), and stack its transpose as a row of the dictionary matrix \(H_\Sigma\):

\[L_s(v)=h_s^T\mathbf v,\qquad \mathbf y=H_M\mathbf u+\boldsymbol\varepsilon,\qquad G=G^T>0,\quad H_M\in\mathbb R^{M\times\mathcal N}.\]

Here \(H_M\) contains only the selected rows, and \(\varepsilon\) is measurement noise. In KF and EnKF the same observation operator is written \(H_k\) when it depends on the time step. The Riesz representation theorem gives the representative \(w_s\) of \(L_s\):

\[G\mathbf w_s=h_s,\qquad L_s(v)=(w_s,v)_X=\mathbf w_s^TG\mathbf v,\qquad \|L_s\|_{(X^{\mathcal N})'}=\sqrt{h_s^T\mathbf w_s}.\]

A row of \(H_\Sigma\) contains functional coefficients; it is not a Riesz representative. The \(\rho_j\) are GEIM interpolation functions, not Riesz representatives either. For an integral sensor \(L_s(v)=\int_\Omega\psi_s v\) in an \(L^2\) metric, boundedness follows from Cauchy–Schwarz with \(\|L_s\|\leq\|\psi_s\|_{L^2}\). Point evaluation is not defined on \(L^2\) equivalence classes. See the average and point-sensor examples before transferring a discrete sensor to a continuous function space. Rescaling a sensor also rescales its noise: changing numerical entries alone does not create more information.

Generating function, interpolation residual and next sensor

Start from the same full-order state snapshots used for reduction. At step \(m\), compute their generalized interpolation residuals

\[e_m(\mu)=u^{\mathcal N}(\mu)-I_m^L u^{\mathcal N}(\mu).\]

The practical searches a finite collection of parameter/sensor pairs:

\[(\mu^{m+1}_{\mathrm{GEIM}},L_{m+1})\in \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\mu\in D_{\mu,\mathrm{train}},\,L\in\Sigma_L}|L(e_m(\mu))|.\]

Then define the generating function, its residual and the normalized new basis function:

\[\xi_{m+1}=u^{\mathcal N}(\mu^{m+1}_{\mathrm{GEIM}}),\qquad r_{m+1}=\xi_{m+1}-I_m^L\xi_{m+1},\qquad \rho_{m+1}=\frac{r_{m+1}}{L_{m+1}(r_{m+1})}.\]

As in EIM, divide by the signed selected value. Provided it is nonzero,

\[L_i(\rho_j)=0\ (i<j),\qquad L_j(\rho_j)=1.\]

Thus \(\mathbb B_m\) is lower triangular with unit diagonal, and the interpolation space \(X_m^{\mathrm{GEIM}}=\operatorname{span}\{\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_m\}\) is hierarchical. The GEIM space is distinct from the Galerkin background space \(X_N\); their dimensions \(m\) and \(N\) need not agree. The maximum sensor response is a dictionary seminorm. Without a norming assumption on the dictionary, it is not the state norm: zero response may mean a nonzero residual is invisible to every candidate sensor.

Algorithm — GEIM on the training fields and sensor dictionary

Let \(F\) contain the training snapshots, \(\mathcal R\) the sampled interpolation functions, and \(p\) the selected sensor indices. These are S, rho and chosen in the notebook.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{GEIM: generating functions and generalized measurements}
\begin{algorithmic}
\INPUT Training fields $F$; normalized dictionary $H_\Sigma$; response tolerance $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{GEIM}}$; budget $M_{\max}$
\STATE $\mathcal R\leftarrow[\,]$; $p\leftarrow[\,]$; $m\leftarrow0$
\WHILE{true}
  \IF{$m=0$}
    \STATE $E\leftarrow F$
  \ELSE
    \STATE $H_m\leftarrow H_\Sigma[p,:]$; $\mathbb B_m\leftarrow H_m\mathcal R$
    \STATE Solve $\mathbb B_m C=H_mF$; $E\leftarrow F-\mathcal R C$
  \ENDIF
  \STATE $D\leftarrow H_\Sigma E$; $(i_*,j_*)\leftarrow\operatorname{argmax}_{i,j}|D_{ij}|$
  \IF{$|D_{i_*j_*}|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{GEIM}}$}
    \RETURN Basis and sensors; dictionary response tolerance met
  \ENDIF
  \IF{$m=M_{\max}$}
    \RETURN Basis and sensors; dimension budget reached
  \ENDIF
  \STATE $\xi_{m+1}\leftarrow F_{:,j_*}$; $r_{m+1}\leftarrow E_{:,j_*}$; $L_{m+1}\leftarrow (H_\Sigma)_{i_*,:}$
  \STATE $\rho_{m+1}\leftarrow r_{m+1}/L_{m+1}(r_{m+1})$
  \STATE Append $\rho_{m+1}$ to $\mathcal R$ and $i_*$ to $p$; $m\leftarrow m+1$
\ENDWHILE
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Approximation error and measurement noise

Define the data-to-state map \(D_M=\mathcal R_M\mathbb B_M^{-1}\) by a linear solve. For \(G=LL^T\), the noise amplification is

\[\|\widehat u(y+\varepsilon)-\widehat u(y)\|_X \leq\|L^TD_M\|_2\|\varepsilon\|_2.\]

The upper-case Cholesky factor \(L\) is a matrix; the \(L_i\) are measurement functionals. The indices and declared types distinguish them. The largest singular value of \(L^TD_M\) computes this norm. For noise \(\varepsilon=C_\varepsilon\zeta\) with covariance \(C_\varepsilon C_\varepsilon^T\), standardized noise is amplified by \(\|L^TD_MC_\varepsilon\|_2\). Noiseless interpolation is the state-to-state map \(I_M^L=D_MH_M\). It reproduces every \(v\in X_M^{\mathrm{GEIM}}\), hence

\[u-I_M^L u=(I-I_M^L)(u-v),\qquad \|u-I_M^L u\|_X\leq (1+\|I_M^L\|_{X\to X})\inf_{v\in X_M^{\mathrm{GEIM}}}\|u-v\|_X.\]

The state-to-state operator norm here is different from the measurement-noise amplification norm above. More interpolation conditions may worsen noise sensitivity. Oversampled least squares and regularization explicitly change the reconstruction rule.

Continue to PBDW and sequential assimilation

GEIM infers coefficients of its interpolation basis from measurements. PBDW instead combines a Galerkin/POD background basis \(Z\) with a correction in the sensor Riesz space. The PBDW chapter denotes the Riesz matrix by \(W\), defined by \(GW=H^T\). Neither \(W\) nor \(Z\) is the GEIM interpolation matrix \(\mathcal R_M\). KF and EnKF retain \(y=Hu+\varepsilon\) and add a time-dependent forecast and covariance update.
